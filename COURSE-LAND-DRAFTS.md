### Lesson 3.2 — What owner financing actually is
> ‹SOURCE — PULL BEFORE PUBLISH: `rX65ABRbEq8` · "How to Buy Land When You're Broke (The Owner Finance Hack)" (the "the owner is the bank," "no credit checks / no income requirement / pay the seller directly through their secured system," low-down/low-monthly framing) + ✏️ new writing for the contract-for-deed vs. note-and-deed-of-trust distinction at a HIGH level only. ⚠️ KEEP THE DON'T-GET-SCREWED SAFETY DEEP-DIVE OUT — that's Module 5. Alright, so what *is* this thing that got a broke guy onto three acres? Let me give it to you plain, no jargon, because the whole industry would rather you found it confusing.

**Owner financing means the owner of the land *is* the bank.** That's the entire idea. Instead of you going to a lender, getting your credit run, proving your income, and putting down a small fortune — the person selling the land just lets you pay *them,* directly, over time. On a site like billyland.com that means **no credit checks, no income requirement, and no massive down payment.** You put a little down, and then every month you pay the seller directly through their secured system, same as you'd pay any other bill, until the land is yours. Some of these run a couple hundred bucks a month with a three-to-five-hundred-dollar down payment — less than some folks pay for a *cell phone,* and a whole lot less than rent in the middle of the city.

Now, there are basically **two flavors** of how owner financing handles the *deed* — that's the piece of paper that proves the land is legally yours — and you should know the difference exists, because it changes *when* you actually hold the title:

- **Contract for deed** (sometimes called a land contract). The seller **keeps the deed in their name until you've finished paying it all off.** You move in, you build, you live your life, you make your payments — but on paper the title doesn't transfer into your name until that last payment clears. You're earning the deed by paying it off.
- **A note plus a deed at closing** (the seller holds a *lien* against the property). Here it's flipped: **you get the deed up front, in your name, right away** — but the seller keeps a legal claim, a lien, recorded against the land until you've paid them in full. You own it on paper from day one; their lien is what guarantees they get paid.

Both can be perfectly legitimate. Both have gotten regular folks onto land they could never have bought through a bank. The difference matters a lot for what happens if life goes sideways mid-contract — and *that's* exactly the kind of thing I'm not going to wave my hands at, so we give it its own careful treatment in Module 5. For now, just file the two shapes away: *deed-at-the-end* versus *deed-up-front-with-a-lien.* When you read a listing and you see how they're structuring it, you'll know which animal you're looking at.

One more honest note, since I never want to sell you the shiny side only: owner-financed land tends to carry **higher interest** than a conventional bank loan. There's a reason for that, and there's a smart way to play it so it costs you a fraction of what they expect — but that's a strategy conversation, and I'm putting it where it belongs, in Module 5, instead of cramming it in here half-baked. Right now the win is just understanding the machine: the owner is the bank, the barrier to entry is low on purpose, and the deed comes to you one of two ways.

**What you'll take:** Owner financing is just "the seller is the bank" — low down, pay monthly, no credit gauntlet — and the deed reaches you one of two ways: held until you finish paying (contract for deed) or handed over up front with a lien on it (note plus deed at closing).

# Module 5 — How Not To Get Screwed (Owner Finance)
*Four lessons. By the end you'll know which kind of deal walks away with your money and which one doesn't — and you'll have a one-page checklist that keeps a stranger from taking your land back after you've paid for it.*

> **One thing before we start, and I'll only say it the once.** I'm a fella who bought land with a handshake and a payment plan, not a lawyer. Everything in here is me handing you what I'd want a neighbor to tell me — it's education, not legal advice. The rules change from state line to state line, and they change *over time*, so before you sign anything with your name on it, you put a real local real-estate attorney between you and that pen. A couple hundred bucks for an hour of their time is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy. Now. Let's talk about it.

### Lesson 5.1 — Who's holding the deed (this is the whole ballgame)
> ‹SOURCE — PULL BEFORE PUBLISH: 🔬 wtpekuah3 owner-finance report · Finding 1 (two structures — contract for deed vs. note + deed of trust; seller keeps legal title vs. buyer gets deed at closing). Verified 3-0.›

Owner financing just means the person selling you the land *is* the bank. No mortgage broker, no credit committee, no fella in a tie deciding you don't make enough. That part is beautiful, and it's how broke people like us get a foot on real dirt. But there are two ways to write that deal up, and they are not the same animal — one of them is a fair shake and the other one is a bear trap with a bow on it. The only thing you have to understand to tell them apart is one question: **when you sign, who walks away holding the deed?**

Way number one — the good one — is a *promissory note secured by a deed of trust or a mortgage.* Fancy words. Here's what it means at the fence post: you sign, and the deed goes in *your* name. That day. You own the land. The seller just gets a lien against it — a legal sticky note that says "he owes me, and if he stops paying I can take it back the long, slow, lawful way." But it's yours. You're the owner from minute one.

Way number two — the trap — is a *contract for deed,* also called a land contract or a land installment contract. Sounds harmless. It is not. Under this one, the seller **keeps** the deed — keeps legal title to the land — until you make the *very last payment.* Until then, all you've got is what the lawyers call equitable title, which is a polite way of saying "a right to mow the grass." You can live there, sweat into it, build on it, pay on it for fifteen years — and on paper you don't own a square inch of it. The seller does. And that gap, between you doing all the owning and them holding all the title, is exactly the gap a bad actor backs the truck up to.

Deed at closing good. Seller-holds-the-deed-till-the-end risky. If you remember nothing else from this whole module, remember which side of that line your deal is on, and fight to be on the good side. Everything else in here is just me showing you *why.*

**What you'll take:** There are two owner-finance structures. In a deed-of-trust/mortgage deal the deed goes in your name at closing — you're the owner immediately. In a contract for deed the seller holds the title until your final payment, and you're just a renter with a receipt. The first one is far safer. Aim for it.

**Your move this week:** If you're already looking at a deal, find out — in writing — which of these two it is. Just ask the seller: "Do I get the deed at closing, or do you hold it till payoff?" Their answer, and how they act when you ask, tells you most of what you need to know.

### Lesson 5.2 — The forfeiture trap (how one missed payment eats fifteen years)
> ‹SOURCE — PULL BEFORE PUBLISH: 🔬 wtpekuah3 owner-finance report · Finding 2 (forfeiture on any default, keep all payments + equity, one missed payment 15 yrs into a 20-yr term; mortgage = 120-day wait under Reg X). Verified 3-0.›

Here's the part that makes me genuinely angry, so bear with me. On a lot of those contract-for-deed deals, there's language buried in there that lets the seller cancel the *whole thing* over **any** default. Not "you stopped paying for a year." Any. One late payment. A lapsed insurance policy because money got tight one bad month. And when they cancel it, they don't just take the land back — they get to keep *every dollar you've ever paid them.* The down payment. Every monthly check. The shed you built, the well you dug, the fence you strung. All of it. Gone. They call it liquidated damages. I call it a stickup with a notary.

And it doesn't matter how close to the finish line you are. Picture a twenty-year contract. You're nineteen years in. You've paid this land off ninety-five percent of the way — you can practically see the deed from your porch. You miss *one* payment. Under the bare, unprotected version of this thing, that seller can cancel the contract, keep the land, keep all nineteen years of your money, and put you out. Nineteen years of your life, gone over one bad month. Ask me how that sits with me.

Now compare that to a regular old mortgage — the thing they tell broke people they can't have. You miss a payment on a mortgage, the law makes the lender *wait one hundred and twenty days* after that first missed payment before they can even *start* foreclosing. And foreclosing isn't a switch they flip — it's notices, and waiting periods, and a public sale, and if the land sells for more than you owed, the extra comes back *to you.* The system that supposedly screws regular folks actually builds in a runway, a brake pedal, a recovery path. The "simple handshake" deal often builds in none of that. Funny how that works.

I want you walking into every one of these conversations knowing that the *default* version of a contract for deed — the one with no extra protections layered on — is the harshest deal in the building. Some states soften it, and that's the next lesson. But you assume the bear trap is set until somebody proves to you it isn't.

**What you'll take:** A bare contract for deed can let the seller cancel over a single missed payment and keep everything you've paid — even decades in. A real mortgage forces a 120-day wait and a formal foreclosure with a public sale and any surplus returned to you. The "simpler" deal is often the one with no mercy built in.

**Your move this week:** If you've got a contract in hand, find the default and forfeiture language — it'll be in there somewhere — and read it out loud. If it lets the seller cancel and keep your money over one missed payment, circle it. That's the first thing your attorney is going to fix or kill.

### Lesson 5.3 — The two ghosts: the hidden mortgage and the missing record
> ‹SOURCE — PULL BEFORE PUBLISH: 🔬 wtpekuah3 owner-finance report · Finding 3 (seller's undisclosed underlying mortgage foreclosed out from under buyer; the $17k surprise-mortgage case) + Finding 4 (¾–⅘ of land contracts unrecorded; Texas study only 20% ever got title; recording prevents subsequent encumbrances). Both verified 3-0.›

There are two ways to lose land you've been faithfully paying on that have nothing to do with you missing a payment. They're the quiet ones. I call them the ghosts because you don't see either one until it's already in the house.

**Ghost number one: the seller doesn't actually own the place free and clear.** Remember — in a contract for deed, *they* hold the title the whole time. Which means they can keep borrowing against it. They can have a mortgage on that land that you know nothing about. And if *they* stop paying *their* loan, the bank forecloses — on land you've been paying off for years — and you can get wiped right out, because their lender was there before you and stands in front of you in line. This isn't a spooky hypothetical. There's a documented case of a woman who made every single payment, finished her contract clean, went to collect the title she'd earned — and it came to her with a *seventeen-thousand-dollar mortgage* stapled to it that she never knew existed and now owed. She did everything right and still got handed somebody else's debt. That's the ghost.

**Ghost number two: the deal never made it into the county records.** Here's a number that ought to make your neck hairs stand up — researchers figure somewhere between *three-quarters and four-fifths* of these land contracts never get recorded at the courthouse at all. They're invisible. As far as the public records know, that land is still 100% the seller's and you're nobody. Which means the seller can turn around and sell it again, or borrow against it again, to somebody who *does* record — and now they're in front of you too. One study down in Texas followed these deals and found only *twenty percent* of buyers ever actually got title. One in five. The other four sweated and paid and walked away with a folder full of canceled checks and no land.

The fix for the second ghost is one beautiful word: **recording.** You take the contract — or a short "memorandum" of it that just says a deal exists — down to the county and you get it stamped into the public record. The second it's recorded, the whole world is on notice that you've got a claim, and the seller can't sell it or borrow against it out from under you *going forward.* Now — and this matters — recording protects you against what happens *next;* it does not erase a mortgage that was already sitting on the land *before* you showed up. That's why recording alone isn't enough. You need the title search from the next lesson to find the ghost that's already in the house, *and* recording to keep new ones from moving in.

**What you'll take:** You can lose paid-for land two ways that aren't your fault — the seller's own hidden mortgage getting foreclosed, and a deal that was never recorded so the seller can sell it twice. Recording the contract stops *future* claims from jumping the line; it doesn't clear an *existing* lien — that takes a title search.

**Your move this week:** Two questions, in writing, for any seller: "Is there any mortgage or lien on this land right now?" and "Will you let me record this contract at the county?" A seller who flinches at recording is telling you something. Believe them.

### Lesson 5.4 — Your home state, and the one-page checklist
> ‹SOURCE — PULL BEFORE PUBLISH: 🔬 wtpekuah3 owner-finance report · Finding 5 (state-by-state forfeiture protections — AZ/FL foreclose like a mortgage; OH no forfeiture after 5 yrs or 20% paid; WA 90-day recorded cure; IA/NC 30-day notice) + Finding 6 (the before-you-sign protections: title search + owner's title insurance, record contract/memorandum, escrow deed w/ neutral third party, attorney review, confirm ownership + liens, amortization schedule, avoid balloons). Verified 3-0 / derived from verified risks. Caveat: state statutes are time-sensitive — buyer MUST verify own state's current law.›

Now here's the good news after three lessons of me scaring you straight: a bunch of states looked at this exact bear trap and said *not in our state, you don't.* The catch is they didn't all say it the same way, and your protection depends entirely on which state that land sits in. I'm going to give you the shape of it — but you are going to **verify your own state's law,** because these are written by legislatures and legislatures love to change things.

Some states said the harsh version flat-out isn't allowed: **Arizona and Florida** make the seller terminate one of these contracts the *same way as a mortgage* — through real foreclosure, with all the process and the public sale. Some states protect you once you've got skin in the game: **Ohio** says if you've paid for *five years* or paid off *twenty percent* of the price, the seller can't just forfeit and evict you — they've got to go to court and foreclose like a bank, Illinois works in that same family. And some states force the seller to give you *notice and a window to fix it* before they can pull the plug: **Iowa** and **North Carolina** require at least a *thirty-day* notice, and **Washington** makes the seller *record* a notice of intent and give you at least a *ninety-day* window to cure — pay up in that window and the contract snaps right back to life (though if a Washington forfeiture does run all the way through, you lose your payments and there's no buying it back, so don't lean on it). And then there are states where the courts still let sellers forfeit fast and ugly with barely any process at all. You need to know which kind of state you're standing in *before* you sign, not after.

So let me hand you the thing this whole module was built to give you. Tape this to the fridge. **Before you sign anything:**

- **Get a title search and buy owner's title insurance.** This is the ghost-hunter from the last lesson. It surfaces any mortgage or lien already on the land, and the insurance pays out if one slips through anyway. On cheap raw land this is the single best dollar you'll spend. Non-negotiable.
- **Record the contract — or a memorandum of it — at the county.** Puts the world on notice you've got a claim. Keeps the seller from selling it or borrowing against it out from under you.
- **Escrow the signed deed with a neutral third party.** A title company or an attorney holds the already-signed deed and is instructed to hand it to *you* automatically when you make that final payment — so getting your title doesn't depend on the seller still being alive, honest, findable, or in a good mood years from now.
- **Have a local real-estate attorney read the whole contract before you sign.** Yes it costs money. It costs a tiny fraction of the land. This is the cheap insurance I mentioned up top.
- **Get written confirmation of exactly what the seller owns and every lien on it.** In writing. Not "nah, it's clean" over the phone.
- **Get a full amortization schedule** — a payment-by-payment chart showing how much goes to principal and interest and exactly when you hit zero. If they can't produce one, ask why.
- **Understand and avoid balloon payments.** That's a deal where the payments look gentle for a few years and then one enormous lump comes due all at once — a lump most of us can't pay, which means we lose the land right at the end. Know if one's hiding in there. If it is, that's a serious talk before you sign.
- **Above all — push for the deed-at-closing structure** from Lesson 5.1. A note and a deed of trust where *you* own it from day one beats a contract for deed every time it's on the table.

And a short list of seller red flags, because the person across the fence tells on themselves if you let them: they don't want to put it in writing; they get cagey or insulted when you ask to record it or run a title search; they push you to sign *today,* fast, before you've had anybody look at it; they can't or won't show you the amortization schedule; they get vague about whether there's an existing mortgage. None of those is automatically a crook. But every one of them is a reason to slow all the way down and get your attorney on the phone.

Here's the heart of it, and then I'll let you go. I would rather you walk away from a deal — even a deal you *want,* even the prettiest three acres you ever stood on — than sign something that lets a stranger take it all back the month before you'd have owned it free and clear. There will be other land. There is always other land. There is not always another nineteen years. Protect yourself first, sign second. Mama didn't raise no quitter, but she didn't raise no sucker either.

**What you'll take:** Your protection depends on your state — some force a real foreclosure (AZ, FL), some protect you once you've built equity (OH: 5 years or 20%), some require notice and a cure window (IA/NC: 30 days, WA: a recorded 90-day cure), and some barely protect you at all. Verify yours. Then run the one-page checklist on every deal, every time.

**Your move this week:** Look up "contract for deed" or "land contract" plus your state's name, and find out which bucket your state is in — then bring that, and this checklist, to a local real-estate attorney before a single signature. That's the whole module, paid forward. I'll catch you in the next one.

*Three lessons. By the end you'll know how a tax auction actually works, why the deed in your hand isn't the clear title you think it is, and how to not get burned bidding on a county's leftovers.*

### Lesson 6.1 — The day I bought the lot next door

### Lesson 6.2 — A deed is not a clear title (read this twice)
> ‹SOURCE — PULL BEFORE PUBLISH: 🔬 tax report `wqge6wma6` — findings: deed-vs-lien taxonomy (high); tax deed does NOT give marketable/insurable title, courts disfavor tax-sale title, quiet-title needed (high); redeemable-deed states let prior owner reclaim even after deed issues (high); CA example — title insurers often won't insure for ~1 year (high, framed as a CA rule not universal). DO NOT teach the refuted specifics: exact TX redemption periods/premiums, IRS 120-day repurchase, CA §3712 survival list.›

Here's the part that'll save you a world of hurt, so I'm going to say it plain: **the piece of paper you win at a tax sale is not the same as owning the land free and clear.** I know. It feels like it should be. You paid, they handed you a deed, done. It is not done.

First thing to get straight — there are two different animals at these sales. A **tax deed** sale hands you a deed to the actual dirt. A **tax lien certificate** sale doesn't give you the land at all — it gives you the right to collect the back taxes the owner owes, with interest, and you'd have to foreclose down the road to maybe end up with the property. Two completely different things wearing similar nametags. Some states do one, some do the other, and some run a hybrid where you get a deed but it comes with strings. Figure out which one your county is running before you do anything else, because "I bought it at the tax sale" can mean two wildly different situations.

Now say you got a real deed. That title is what the lawyers politely call **clouded.** It works like this: the whole deed is only good if the county followed its own rules to the letter when they took the land and sold it. Miss a notice, botch a deadline, and the sale can get unwound. Courts don't *like* tax-sale titles — they squint at 'em. So most of the time, to turn that clouded deed into title you can actually insure, build on, and sell, you've got to go file something called a **quiet-title action** — basically a lawsuit that asks a judge to bless the sale and wipe out everybody else's claims. That costs money and it costs months. Budget for it now, not later.

And here's the gut-punch. In a bunch of states — the "redeemable deed" states — the person who *lost* the land to taxes can still come back and **redeem** it, paying you off and taking it back, even after that deed's been issued and recorded. So you could win, start dreaming about your garden, and get redeemed right out from under it. On top of that, title companies are no fools — in plenty of places they flat *won't* sell you title insurance on a tax-auction parcel for a good while after the sale, sometimes about a year, because they're waiting to see if anybody challenges it. In California, for instance, the sale can get contested for up to a year, and insurers tend to sit on their hands that whole time. That's a California rule, not the law of the land everywhere — but the *flavor* of it shows up all over: you do not own this thing clean on day one.

So what does that mean for you, the homesteader who just wants to move on and start? It means **don't pour a foundation, don't build the cabin, don't sink your savings into improvements** until you know your state's redemption window is closed and your title's been cleared. Improving land that can still be yanked back is how you donate a deck to a stranger.

**What you'll take:** Tax deed buys you a fight, not a finish line. The deed is clouded, the prior owner may still be able to redeem in some states, and you usually can't insure or safely build until a quiet-title action (and any waiting period) clears it — often months to a year out. Plan for that gap or it'll eat you.

**Your move this week:** Call or search your target county and find out two things: (1) is it a tax **deed** or tax **lien** state, and (2) is there a **redemption period** after the sale where the old owner can take it back, and how long is it? Write both answers down. That's the difference between a plan and a gamble.

### Lesson 6.3 — Sold as-is, and "as-is" hides teeth
> ‹SOURCE — PULL BEFORE PUBLISH: 🔬 tax report `wqge6wma6` — AS-IS / no-warranty is the single most consistent finding across every county (high); federal/IRS liens and governmental liens CAN survive a tax sale (medium — general principle, frame as "can," verify locally); landlocked/unbuildable parcels not warranted (medium). 🎥 `kzNtTVrgIwc` (his own as-is purchase). DO NOT state the refuted IRS 120-day or §3712 specifics as fact.›

There is one line in every single one of these tax-auction listings, and it is the most honest thing the county will ever tell you: **sold as-is.** No warranty. Not about the condition, not about the title, not about whether you can build on it, not about whether anything nasty is buried in it, and not about whether there's a legal way to even *drive* to it. The county is selling you a question mark and charging you for the privilege. They will say so, in all caps, and they mean it.

So "as-is" sounds like used-car-lot boilerplate, but on raw land it's got teeth. A few of the ones that bite:

**Surviving liens.** Clearing the property taxes doesn't automatically scrub *every* debt off the land. Some liens — including, in some situations, **federal/IRS liens** and certain government liens — can ride right through the sale and land in your lap. Whether one survives depends on the type of lien and whether the right parties got dragged into the process, and that's genuinely state-and-situation specific. The move isn't to memorize a rulebook I'd be making up — it's to pull a title search on the parcel *before* you bid and have a title pro tell you what's actually attached to it.

**Landlocked and unbuildable.** Cheap is cheap for a reason, and sometimes the reason is there's no legal road to it, or it's a sliver, or a drainage ditch, or a patch you physically cannot build on. The county does not promise you access, zoning, or buildability. They'll happily sell you a parcel you can never legally reach. (We go deep on landlocked in the next module — for now just know the county won't warn you.)

This is why you do the boring work *before* the auction, not after: pull the title and chain of ownership, look for liens that survive, confirm there's recorded legal access, check zoning and whether you can build, and — since there's no inspection and no walkthrough — go put your boots on the actual ground if you possibly can.

⚖️ **The disclaimer, in plain English:** I'm a guy who bought land at a tax sale, not your lawyer. This module is me teaching you the lay of the land so you ask the right questions — it is **not** legal advice. Every bit of this varies by state and county: which liens survive, how long the redemption period runs, whether you even get a deed. Before you bid a single dollar, **verify it with that specific county and run it past a local real estate attorney and a title company.** That conversation costs a lot less than a parcel you can't use.

**What you'll take:** "As-is" means *every* risk is yours — surviving liens, no access, can't-build, the works — and the county owes you nothing. Your protection isn't trust, it's homework: title search, lien check, access and zoning check, eyes on the dirt, done before you ever bid.

**Your move this week:** Pick one real parcel off a county tax-sale list and play detective on it — pull up the APN, find it on a map, and write down three things you genuinely can't answer yet (Is there legal access? Any surviving liens? Can you build?). Those three unknowns are exactly what a title company and a quick attorney call exist to answer. Practicing on a parcel you're *not* buying is how you get good before it's your money on the clock.

# Module 7 — Don't Get Burned
*Four lessons. By the end you'll know how to vet a parcel for the three things that quietly ruin cheap land — water, access, and waste — plus the one truth about codes that nobody explains: state law, the national code, and the local guy named Phil are three different things.*

### Lesson 7.1 — Water: how far down, and whose is it anyway
> ‹SOURCE — PULL BEFORE PUBLISH: 🎥 `rAlBN8wisu0` · "Buying Cheap Land - AVOID THESE MISTAKES" — Mistake #1: ~400 ft well, $50k quote ("almost three times what we paid"), rainwater + hauling 5-gal jugs, set the homestead back years, cut their food production. The fix: call neighbors + county, pull well permits, average depths on a topo map. 🔬 reg report `wp72z7psv` — riparian (East, reasonable use) vs prior-appropriation (West, first-in-time) (high); wells state-regulated, licensing differs e.g. AL licensed driller vs TX none on own land (high).›

Mistake number one, and I made it myself, so settle in. When we first bought, we were so wrapped up in the *problems* we came out here to solve that we forgot the boring practical stuff. Like water. We got here, fell in love with how gorgeous it all was, and *then* went to get a quote on drilling a well. Turns out a well here is almost **400 feet deep.** The driller's number? Around **fifty grand.** Which was almost *three times* what we paid for the property in the first place. Carrie took that one a little harder than I did.

We made it work — rainwater catchment, and for *years* we drove down to a local campground every other day and filled up 5-gallon jugs just to cover basic needs. Inconvenient? Sure. But the real cost is this: it cut way down on how much food we could grow for ourselves. That one oversight set this homestead back *years.* All because I didn't ask one question before I signed.

So don't be me. Two layers to water, and you check both before you buy.

**Layer one: can you physically get it, and what's it cost?** Talk to the neighbors — ask how deep *their* wells are. Then call the county, and if you're genuinely nice to the folks who answer (they spend all day getting yelled at, so be the bright spot), they'll often pull a few well permits and water reports for parcels near yours. Drop those depths on a topographical map, average 'em out, and you've got a real estimate of how deep — and how expensive — your well is going to be. Then call the driller back with actual numbers. That one phone call can change your whole opinion of a property before it's too late.

**Layer two: whose water is it, legally?** This one nobody tells the dreamers. Water *rights* aren't the same everywhere. Broadly, Eastern states run on **riparian "reasonable use"** — water rights are tied to land that touches the water, and you're entitled to reasonable use of it. Western states run on **prior appropriation** — "first in time, first in right," meaning the water can be spoken for by whoever claimed it first, regardless of whose land it crosses, and a few Western states are a hybrid of both. On top of that, **wells are state-regulated**, and the rules genuinely differ — some states require you to use a licensed driller, others let you drill on your own property yourself. Where your prospective parcel sits on that map decides what you're actually allowed to do with the water under and around it. Don't assume. Verify with the state water agency and the county.

**What you'll take:** Water can quietly cost more than the land and limit everything you grow. Before you buy, nail down *both* how deep/expensive a well is (neighbors + county well permits + a topo map) *and* what your state's water-rights system lets you legally do (riparian East vs. prior-appropriation West, plus state well rules).

**Your move this week:** For a parcel you're eyeing, do two calls: ask a neighbor how deep their well is, and ask the county to pull a couple of nearby well permits. Then look up whether your target state is riparian or prior-appropriation. Three answers, one afternoon — and you'll know more than most people who already bought.

### Lesson 7.2 — Access: a handshake is not a road
> ‹SOURCE — PULL BEFORE PUBLISH: 🎥 `rAlBN8wisu0` — Mistake #5: emergency/access during washouts, fires, ambulances; "is there only one way in or out"; the POA-maintains-the-roads bit ($80/yr, read the CC&Rs). 🔬 reg report `wp72z7psv` — landlocked = no recorded easement = no legal access; informal permission is a revocable license, not legal access (high, "most decisive off-grid check"); easement by necessity needs prior unity of ownership + necessity at severance, may require litigation (high). DO NOT teach the refuted "no-code county still requires septic/well" item — that's Lesson 7.3/7.4 territory and it's on the do-not-rely list.›

Cheapest land on the list is real often the landlocked stuff, so let's talk about getting *to* your property — legally and physically, because those are two different problems.

Here's the one that sinks people: a parcel can be **landlocked**, meaning there's no recorded legal way to reach it from a public road. And "but the neighbor said I could just use his driveway" does *not* fix that. Informal permission — a handshake, a friendly "sure, go ahead" — is what the law calls a **revocable license.** Translation: he can take it back any day he wants, or worse, the *next* owner can, and now you own ground you can't legally drive to. A favor is not access. If access matters to you, and it does, it has to be a **recorded easement** — written down, filed at the county, attached to the land itself so it survives whoever owns the neighboring parcel. Get it recorded. A handshake is not a road.

Now, if a parcel's already landlocked, there's sometimes a legal path called an **easement by necessity** — but don't bank on it like it's a sure thing. It generally requires that your parcel and the neighbor's were once *one* property under *one* owner and got split in a way that left yours stranded with no other way out. And even when the elements are there, you may have to go to court to actually get it. That's a lawsuit and a maybe, not a guarantee. Far better to confirm recorded access *before* you buy than to gamble on winning one after.

And access isn't just "can I get in on a sunny day." This is the mistake that's genuinely life-or-death. Walk or drive *every* route in and out. What happens to that dirt road after it rains for three days straight — is it a washed-out trench? Is there only one way in, or have you got a backup if the main road's gone? Can an *ambulance* reach you? Can a fire truck? Can you get *out* when a wildfire's got everybody on that same road trying to leave at once? Most folks fret over title insurance and never think about the fact that the romantic, secluded, end-of-the-world parcel can become a trap the day you actually need help.

One more, and it'll surprise you: don't auto-reject a property just because it's got an HOA or a **POA** (property owners association). Out here, plenty of those exist for exactly *one* reason — maintaining the roads in and out, including for emergencies. Ours runs about 80 bucks a year, and that money keeps the roads passable so my family can always get out whether I'm home or not. Before you treat a POA like a curse, get the **CC&Rs** (the recorded rules) and actually *read* them. Find out what it can and can't make you do. Sometimes that's a diamond in the rough, not a deal-breaker.

**What you'll take:** Legal access and physical access are two separate checks, and both can kill you — one financially, one literally. Demand a *recorded* easement (never a handshake), confirm there's more than one way out for emergencies, and read the CC&Rs before you judge a POA.

**Your move this week:** For a parcel you're considering, find out one thing first: is there a **recorded easement** giving it legal access to a public road? Pull the deed or ask a title company. If the answer is "the seller says you can use the road," that's not a yes — that's a red flag.

### Lesson 7.3 — The thing everyone forgets: where the poo goes
> ‹SOURCE — PULL BEFORE PUBLISH: 🎥 `yOSqgvrLEwI` · "Living Off Grid: What Everyone Forgets to Solve" — trash/junk/poo; his ~$200 IBC-tote septic for the mother-in-law; DIY standard septic ~$3k / pro $10-15k; composting throne, "put the bin away from the house, ask me how I know," biosolids. 🔬 reg report `wp72z7psv` — most jurisdictions still require a septic OPTION so land must pass a PERC test (high); composting-toilet rules vary, few allow as sole system, NSF/ANSI 41 often required (high); rainwater legal in all 50 states, CO most restrictive — two barrels / 110 gal / no indoor use (high). DO NOT teach refuted: "code requires a flush toilet for habitable dwelling," and don't state "no-code counties still require septic permits" as fact.›

This is the subject so important it gets its own jingle, and it's the one *everyone* skips when they're daydreaming about the land: waste. Specifically, the poo. You can grow all the tomatoes you want — you still have to answer the question of where it all goes, and you'd best answer it *before* you own the dirt, not after.

Here's the part that can make a parcel unbuildable, so listen up: in most places, even way out in the sticks, the county still wants you to have a **septic option** on the property. And to put in a septic system, the land has to pass a **perc test** — a percolation test where they check whether your soil actually drains the way septic needs it to. If that ground won't perc, the county may not green-light a septic system, and a parcel you can't legally put waste-handling on is a parcel you may not be able to legally live on. That's how a beautiful, cheap lot turns into a paperweight. So when you're vetting land, *ask the county health department whether the parcel can pass a perc test* — or whether one's already on file.

Now, the systems themselves, from my own experience:

When Carrie's mother came to live out here, I cobbled together a septic system out of an old IBC tote and about a hundred feet of leach line. Cost about **200 bucks**, and for one or two people it works great. Want a proper standard septic for a whole family? If you do it yourself and buy the materials, you're looking at around **$3,000.** Have it professionally installed and it's more like **$10,000 to $15,000.** There are hundreds of how-to videos out there.

We've also run a **composting throne** for years now. They're low-tech, so not much goes wrong, and they don't drink up water when water's scarce — which, if you saw the last lesson, matters. But know two things the other videos won't tell you straight. One: composting-toilet *rules* vary a ton by state, lots of places don't have clean rules for them, very few will let a composting toilet be your *only* system, and there's often a standard (NSF/ANSI 41) the unit's supposed to meet — so it usually doesn't get you *out* of needing that septic option. Two, from personal experience: put the composting *bin* a good distance from the house. When it's hot out and you've just emptied the tank, it *stinks.* Ask me how I know.

And before you decide that using composted humanure on growing ground is the grossest thing you've ever heard — about half the sewage in this country gets treated and turned into fertilizer that grows food. Look up the word **biosolids.** Coin-flip's odds you're already eating off it.

**What you'll take:** Waste is the silent deal-killer. Most counties still require a septic *option*, which means the land has to **pass a perc test** — so a parcel that won't perc can be unbuildable. Composting toilets are great but rarely count as your *only* legal system. Check perc *before* you buy, and price your septic honestly ($200 cobbled to $15k professional).

**Your move this week:** Call the county health department about a parcel you're eyeing and ask one question: can this land pass a perc test for septic, or is there a perc/septic record on file? That answer tells you whether you've found a homestead or a very expensive campsite.

### Lesson 7.4 — Codes vs. enforcement: state law, the national code, and Phil
> ‹SOURCE — PULL BEFORE PUBLISH: 🔬 reg report `wp72z7psv` — KEY FINDING (medium, 2-1): no single national building code; states/localities adopt model codes (NEC, IRC) editions individually; some rural counties effectively no-code via state-default fallback; local AHJ enforcement laxer in unincorporated areas. 🎥 `rAlBN8wisu0` (the "governments are the worst at everything," local-county-board-makes-rules texture) for tone. ⚠️ DO-NOT-RELY LIST — do NOT teach: specific state NEC editions, the "no statewide NEC adoption" state list, "code requires a flush toilet," "no-code counties still require septic/well permits," "tiny-home-on-wheels = RV occupancy ban." Teach the PRINCIPLE + "verify your county." ✏️ Jason's "state law → national code → the local guy named Phil" spine.›

Alright, the big one. People ask "is it legal to build/live off-grid?" like there's one answer printed in a book somewhere. There isn't. So let me give you the mental model that actually works, and it's got three layers: **state law, the national code, and the local guy named Phil who comes out to take a look.**

**Layer one — there is no single national building code.** I know that's not what it feels like. But the United States doesn't have one master rulebook that everybody runs on. What exists are *model* codes — things like the IRC for residential building and the NEC for electrical — written by national outfits as a template. Nobody's bound by them until somebody officially *adopts* them.

**Layer two — states and localities adopt those codes piecemeal, edition by edition.** A state or a county picks up a model code and says "this one's ours now." Different places adopt different versions at different times, and some local areas haven't adopted their own at all — in which case they might fall back to whatever the state has on the books, *if* the state has one. The practical upshot: some rural counties end up **effectively no-code**, and others are strict, and there's no way to know which from your couch. You have to ask the specific county. (And I'm not going to recite you a list of which states run which edition — those lists go stale, they get quoted wrong all over the internet, and the only edition that matters is the one *your* county is enforcing *right now.*)

**Layer three — Phil.** This is the layer the dreamers never see coming. A code sitting on the books is not the same as a code getting *enforced.* Enforcement is done by the local **AHJ** — the Authority Having Jurisdiction, which is a fancy way of saying the local building/permit department. And out in the **unincorporated** parts of a county — the stretches that aren't inside any city limits — enforcement tends to be a whole lot lighter than it is in town. Whether you need a permit, whether anybody inspects, whether anybody ever drives out to look — that comes down to that local office, and in a lot of places, that comes down to one human being. State law tells you what *could* apply. The national code tells you the *template.* But what actually happens on your dirt comes down to Phil — the local inspector who shows up, looks around, and decides. Phil is the layer that matters, and Phil is different in every county.

You saw what this looks like in practice in an earlier lesson — a local county board out here decided to regulate the pot grows, made up a rule, the growers found the cheapest possible way around it, the board made *another* rule. Governments gonna government. The lesson isn't "codes are evil," it's that the rules are *local, specific, and live* — they're made and enforced by the particular county you're standing in, not by some clean national standard you can look up once and trust forever.

So here's the only reliable move: **before you buy, call that county's planning/building department and its health department and just ask.** Do you require a building permit out here? Do you inspect? Can I live in an RV or a camper while I build, and for how long? What do I need for septic and a well? Ask the actual AHJ for the actual parcel. Whatever some video (including this one) tells you about "no-code counties" or what's allowed — verify it against the real office, because that's the only answer that can ever bite you.

A couple of true things worth knowing while you're at it: **rainwater catchment is legal in all 50 states** — there's no federal ban — though it's state-regulated, and Colorado's the most restrictive of the bunch (last we checked, a tight cap on how much you can collect). And as I said in the waste lesson, most places still want a septic *option* and a perc test. Beyond those, assume nothing and ask the county.

⚖️ **The disclaimer, plainly:** I'm a homesteader who learned this the hard and dumb way, not a lawyer or a code official. This module is here to make you ask sharper questions — it is **not** legal advice. Building codes, zoning, water rights, septic and access rules vary enormously by state *and* by county, and what's true two counties over may be flat wrong on your parcel. Before you buy or build, **verify everything with that specific county's planning, building, and health departments, and run the big stuff past a local real estate attorney and title professional.** It's the cheapest insurance you'll ever buy.

**What you'll take:** "Is it legal?" has no national answer. Codes are a template that states/counties adopt piecemeal, some rural counties are effectively no-code, and what actually happens on your land is decided by the local AHJ — Phil — whose enforcement is laxer out in unincorporated areas. Don't trust a list off the internet. Call the county.

**Your move this week:** Call the planning/building department for the county you want to live in and ask three questions: Do you require a building permit out here? Can I live in an RV or camper while I build, and for how long? What do I need for septic and a well? Write down what *that office* tells you — that's your real rulebook, not anything you read online.
